\section{Introduction}

Computing distance on curved surfaces presents a fundamental challenge distinct from Euclidean geometry. A key distinction exists between \textit{graph distance} (Dijkstra), which constrains paths to mesh edges, and true \textit{geodesic distance}, which is defined as the shortest path along the continuous surface. Graph-based methods systematically overestimate distances, while the multiple-source shortest path problem remains critical for shape analysis and path planning.

\paragraph{Existing Approaches.}
Before the Heat Method, two families dominated:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
    \sloppy 
    \item \textbf{Propagation Methods (Fast Marching):} These solve the Eikonal equation $|\nabla \phi| = 1$ by propagating a wavefront. While reaching $O(n \log n)$, they are inherently serial, which hinders parallelization, and they lack reusability because every new source requires a full re-computation.
    
    \item \textbf{Exact Polyhedral Methods (MMP):} These compute exact paths across faces. While precise, they are computationally expensive ($O(n^2 \log n)$), complex to implement, and restricted to triangle meshes without generalization to point clouds.
\end{itemize}

\smallskip
% --- CHANGE 1: Abstract/Intro Update ---
\noindent This report presents a comprehensive review of the \textbf{Heat Method} [Crane et al. 2013] and its generalization, the \textbf{Vector Heat Method} [Sharp et al. 2019]. These works overcome the limitations of previous propagation and polyhedral methods by reformulating distance and transport problems as solutions to linear partial differential equations (PDEs), allowing for pre-factorization and millisecond-scale query times.

\section{Methodology A - The Scalar Heat Method}

The core contribution of this project relies on the \textit{Heat Method} for geodesic distance computation. Unlike graph-based methods (e.g., Dijkstra) that are limited to edge paths, or PDE-based methods (e.g., Eikonal solvers) that are often non-linear and difficult to implement on curved domains, the Heat Method solves the geodesic distance problem using standard linear algebra.

The method is based on Varadhan's formula, which establishes a link between the heat kernel $k_{t}(x, y)$ and the geodesic distance $d(x, y)$:
\begin{equation}
    \lim_{t \to 0} - \frac{\sqrt{4t}}{2} \ln k_{t}(x, y) = d(x, y)
\end{equation}
While Varadhan's formula theoretically requires $t \to 0$, numerical issues arise with vanishing time steps. The Heat Method circumvents this by utilizing the heat kernel only to determine the \textit{direction} of the geodesic, rather than its magnitude.

\subsection{The SHM Algorithm}
The method proceeds in three distinct steps, transforming the problem into two linear Poisson-like systems and one local normalization step.

\paragraph{1. Integrate the Heat Flow.}
The heat equation $\dot{u} = \Delta u$ is first solved for a fixed source point $x_0$ for a short time $t$. This describes how heat diffuses over the surface:
\begin{equation}
    (M - t L_C) u = \delta_{x_0}
\end{equation}
Here, $\delta_{x_0}$ is the Kronecker delta (heat source), and a single backward Euler step is utilized for unconditional stability.

\paragraph{2. Evaluate the Vector Field.}
The heat kernel provides a smooth gradient field. The gradient of the heat distribution, $\nabla u$, points towards the heat source (steepest descent). Therefore, the vector field $X$ pointing \textit{away} from the source along geodesics is the normalized negative gradient:
\begin{equation}
    X = - \frac{\nabla u}{\| \nabla u \|}
\end{equation}
This step is performed locally per face. By normalizing the gradient, the incorrect magnitude provided by the heat kernel (which shrinks too fast) is discarded while the correct geodesic direction is preserved.

\paragraph{3. Solve the Poisson Equation.}
Finally, a scalar field $\phi$ (the distance function) whose gradient best matches $X$ is sought. The Poisson equation is solved:
\begin{equation}
    L_C \phi = \nabla \cdot X
\end{equation}
where $\nabla \cdot X$ is the divergence of the normalized vector field. The solution $\phi$ represents the geodesic distance from $x_0$ to all vertices.

\subsection{Discretization \& Robustness}
To implement this on a triangle mesh, the \textbf{Cotangent Laplacian} $L_C$ is utilized. For an edge connecting vertices $i$ and $j$, the weight is given by:
\begin{equation}
    w_{ij} = \frac{1}{2} (\cot \alpha_{ij} + \cot \beta_{ij})
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{ij}$ and $\beta_{ij}$ are the angles opposite the edge. This is combined with the lumped Mass matrix $M$, a diagonal matrix where $M_{ii}$ is one-third of the area of all triangles incident to vertex $i$.

% --- CHANGE 2: Numerical Considerations ---
\paragraph{Numerical Considerations.}
Crane et al. recommend a time step of $t = h^2$, where $h$ is the mean edge length, noting that this choice is invariant to scale and refinement. The method strictly requires Neumann boundary conditions ($\nabla u \cdot \mathbf{n} = 0$) to conserve heat within the domain; Dirichlet conditions would cause artificial heat loss at boundaries, warping the gradient field. A key performance benefit is the ability to pre-factor the symmetric positive-definite system matrices using sparse Cholesky decomposition, reducing subsequent queries to efficient back-substitution.

\section{Methodology B - The Vector Heat Method}

\subsection{From Scalar to Vector}

While the Scalar Heat Method computes a distance field by diffusing a scalar function (heat magnitude), the Vector Heat Method (VHM) generalizes this to tangent vector fields. The central challenge in curved space is that vectors cannot simply be averaged like scalars because they live in different tangent planes.

To solve this, Sharp et al. rely on a \textbf{decoupling principle}: they separate the vector field into its \textit{direction} and its \textit{magnitude}. The direction is computed by diffusing vectors via parallel transport, while the magnitude is corrected using standard scalar diffusion.

\paragraph{Parallel Transport and Rotations.}
In Euclidean space, vectors can be compared directly. On a curved surface, however, moving a vector requires \textit{parallel transport}, which involves sliding the vector along a path (geodesic) such that its angle relative to the path remains constant. Due to curvature (holonomy), a vector transported around a loop may not return to its original orientation. The VHM must account for these rotations induced by geometry.

\subsection{Mathematical Formulation}

To implement parallel transport numerically, we need a way to represent tangent vectors and their rotations efficiently.

\paragraph{Complex Number Representation.}
Since a tangent plane on a surface is a 2D vector space, it is isomorphic to the complex plane $\mathbb{C}$. We can therefore encode a tangent vector at vertex $i$ as a single complex number $U_i$. 
This representation simplifies rotations: rotating a vector by an angle $\theta$ corresponds to simple multiplication by $e^{i\theta}$.
\[
U \in \mathbb{C}^n , \qquad U_i \in T_{v_i}\mathcal{M}\simeq\mathbb{C}.
\]

\paragraph{The Connection Laplacian ($L^\nabla$).} 
To diffuse these complex vectors, we cannot use the standard Laplacian, which treats values as scalars. We replace it with the \textbf{Connection Laplacian}, which explicitly accounts for the rotation $r_{ij}$ required to transport a vector from vertex $i$ to vertex $j$.
\[
(L^{\nabla})_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
-w_{ij} \bar{r}_{ij} & \text{if } i \neq j \text{ and } (i,j) \in E,\\
\sum_{k} w_{ik} & \text{if } i=j,\\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
Here, $r_{ij} \in \mathbb{C}$ (with $|r_{ij}|=1$) is the unitary rotation between tangent spaces. This operator allows us to "smooth" vector fields while respecting the curvature of the manifold.

\subsection{The VHM Algorithm}

The algorithm reconstructs the transported vector field by solving three linear systems. The logic is to (1) find the average direction using the Connection Laplacian, (2) find the correct magnitude using the Scalar Laplacian, and (3) combine them.

\paragraph{1. Compute Directions (Vector Diffusion).}
First, we diffuse the initial vector field $X$ for a short time $t$. This averages the \textit{directions} to find a smooth flow:
\[
(M - t\,L^\nabla)\,U = U_0.
\]
The resulting field $U$ points in the right direction, but its magnitude is incorrect; it decays too quickly due to diffusion.

\paragraph{2. Compute Magnitudes (Scalar Diffusion).}
To recover the correct magnitude, we look at how a scalar mass would diffuse. We define the initial magnitude $u_0 = |X|$ and solve the standard scalar heat equation:
\[
(M - t\,L_C)\,u = u_0.
\]
This gives us a reference scalar field $u$ representing the "amount" of vector that should exist at every point.

\paragraph{3. Normalize the Source.}
Since the source is discrete, we also need to account for the diffusion density. We solve a second scalar system for the indicator function $\phi_0 = \mathbf{1}_{X\neq0}$:
\[
(M - t\,L_C)\,\phi = \phi_0.
\]

\paragraph{4. Recombination.}
Finally, we construct the solution $\bar{X}$. We take the \textit{direction} from the vector solution $U$ and scale it by the \textit{magnitude} ratio derived from the scalar solutions:
\[
\bar X = \underbrace{\frac{u}{\phi}}_{\text{Correct Magnitude}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\frac{U}{|U|}}_{\text{Correct Direction}}.
\]
This elegant recombination yields a vector field that is parallel transported across the entire surface in a globally consistent manner.

\subsection{Applications}

The ability to transport vectors efficiently opens the door to fundamental geometric operations that were previously computationally prohibitive. Two key applications demonstrated by Sharp et al. are the computation of the \textit{Logarithmic Map} and the \textit{Karcher Mean}.

\subsubsection{The Logarithmic Map}

To perform statistics on a curved surface, one often needs to "flatten" the geometry locally around a base point $x$. The \textbf{Logarithmic Map} $\log_x(y)$ assigns to every point $y$ a tangent vector at $x$ whose length equals the geodesic distance $d(x,y)$ and whose direction points along the geodesic toward $y$.

Computing this typically requires "shooting" rays (solving ODEs) for every target point. The VHM circumvents this by recognizing that the log map is simply a polar coordinate system $(r, \varphi)$:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item The radial coordinate $r$ is the geodesic distance (computed via the Scalar Heat Method).
    \item The angular coordinate $\varphi$ is recovered by diffusing a reference vector field using the Connection Laplacian.
\end{itemize}
This allows the log map to be computed for \textit{all} points on the mesh in a single pass.

\begin{algorithm}[t!]
\caption{\textbf{Logarithmic Map via VHM}}
\label{alg:logmap-vhm}
\small % Reduce font size to save space
\textbf{Description.}  
Recovers polar coordinates by combining scalar distance ($r$) and vector transport ($\varphi$).

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Input:} Base point $x\in\mathcal{M}$; arbitrary reference vector $X_0 \in T_x\mathcal{M}$.\\
\textbf{Output:} Log map vectors $v_i \in T_x\mathcal{M}$ for all vertices $i$.

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State \textbf{Radial:} Compute distance $r$ using the Scalar Heat Method;
  \State \textbf{Angular:} Diffuse $X_0$ via VHM to obtain field $U$;
  \State For every vertex $i$, compute the angle $\varphi_i$ of $U_i$ relative to $X_0$;
  \State Construct the log vector: $v_i = r_i \cdot e^{i \varphi_i}$.
  \State \Return vectors $v$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Geometric Centers (Karcher Means)}

A direct application of the Logarithmic Map is the computation of an "average" point on a curved surface. In Euclidean space, the mean minimizes the sum of squared distances. The generalization of this concept to manifolds is the \textbf{Karcher Mean} (or Fréchet mean), defined as the point $x$ that minimizes:
\[
E(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n d(x, y_i)^2.
\]
Minimizing this energy requires an iterative gradient descent. Since we cannot linearly add points on a manifold, we instead calculate the "error vectors" pointing towards each data point using the Log Map, average them in the tangent plane, and move the estimate in that direction.

Because the VHM computes the Log Map globally and efficiently, it enables the rapid computation of Karcher means even for large datasets on complex topology.

\begin{algorithm}[t!]
\caption{\textbf{Karcher Mean via VHM}}
\label{alg:karcher-mean}
\small
\textbf{Description.}
Iteratively moves the center point $m$ in the direction of the average log-vector until convergence.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Input:} Target points $y_1,\dots,y_n$; step size $\tau \in (0,1]$.\\
\textbf{Output:} Karcher mean $m \in \mathcal{M}$.

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State Initialize estimate $m^{(0)}$ (e.g., at random);
  \Repeat
    \State Compute Log Maps $v_i = \log_{m^{(k)}}(y_i)$ for all $y_i$ using VHM;
    \State Compute average direction: $\bar{v} = \frac{1}{n}\sum v_i$;
    \State Move estimate: $m^{(k+1)} = \exp_{m^{(k)}}(\tau \bar{v})$;
  \Until{$\|\bar{v}\|$ is below tolerance}
  \State \Return $m$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Experiment A: Parameter Sensitivity}
The authors claim that a time step of $t = h^2$ is optimal. To evaluate this, we measured the deviation of the Heat Method against **Graph Dijkstra** distance across a range of time multipliers $m \in [0.25, 64]$. 

\textbf{Note on Baseline:} We selected Graph Dijkstra as a reference specifically because it is parameter-free. Although it represents an upper bound on the true distance, it is algorithmically stable. The critical finding is not the absolute error, but the volatility: while the Dijkstra baseline remains fixed, the Heat Method's output fluctuates wildly based on $t$, disproving the claim of parameter robustness.

\section{Discussion \& Limitations}

\subsection{Boundary Physics (Experiment D)}
A fundamental limitation of the Heat Method is its reliance on physical boundary conditions that distort the geometry near edges. We tested this on a flat square mesh, where true geodesic isolines should be perfectly circular arcs.

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:boundary_bias}, the physical behavior of heat diffusion creates metric bias. The standard Neumann condition treats the mesh boundary as an insulating wall. This prevents heat from crossing the edge, forcing the gradient field to flow parallel to the boundary. Consequently, the distance isolines flatten out to become perpendicular to the edge. This makes the method topologically rigid compared to graph-based methods, which are unaffected by the boundary shape until the path physically reaches it.

\subsection{Precision vs. Stability Trade-off}
Synthesizing our experiments, we conclude the Heat Method is best viewed as a **geometric approximation** rather than an exact solver.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Pros:} It is unconditionally stable on bad geometry (Exp B) and incredibly fast for static queries (Exp C).
    \item \textbf{Cons:} It systematically biases the metric near boundaries (Exp D) and requires large diffusion times to overcome discretization noise (Exp A), acting as a low-pass filter that erodes high-frequency geometric features.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

This report analyzed the Heat Method and Vector Heat Method, which reformulate geodesic distance computation from a non-linear propagation problem into a linear heat diffusion problem. By leveraging sparse linear algebra, these methods allow complex geometric tasks to be solved efficiently. The primary innovation lies in the ability to pre-factor the Laplacian matrix, enabling millisecond-scale query times for static geometry. \\

However, our experimental stress tests reveal that this efficiency comes with specific trade-offs. We found that the method acts as a geometric low-pass filter that achieves stability on noisy meshes by smoothing over high-frequency details, which inherently sacrifices metric precision. Furthermore, the reliance on diffusion physics introduces bias near open boundaries where the Neumann condition distorts the distance field. Finally, while the method is exceptionally fast for static shapes, the overhead of matrix factorization makes it slower than propagation methods for dynamic meshes. \\

In summary, the Heat Method is a specialized approximation technique rather than a universal replacement for exact solvers. It is the optimal choice for shape analysis and retrieval tasks where speed and smoothness are paramount. For applications requiring strict metric accuracy or real-time deformation, exact polyhedral or fast marching methods remain the superior tools.
